Childcare sharing and parental well-being

A time diary perspective

Author

Heejung Chung and Pierre Walthéry

Published

18/12/2025

Abstract

The division of childcare between mothers and fathers has traditionally been studied in relation to the parental relationship and children’s subjective well-being (SWB). However, its direct impact on parental SWB remains underexplored. This gap may stem from limitations in existing research on fathers’ involvement, including a lack of differentiation between types of childcare, reliance on simplified time-use measures, and the predominance of cognitive instruments (e.g., life satisfaction) to assess SWB - measures that are less responsive to day-to-day fluctuations. Using a subsample of dual-earning households data from the 2015 UK Time Use Survey for a subsample of dual-earning households, this study models the relationship between the specific share of various types of childcare performed by fathers and the daily enjoyment levels of parents, a more immediate and affective indicator of SWB than cognitive ones. Control variables included the day of the week (weekdays vs. weekends) and the total time fathers spentd on childcare. The findings reveal an asymmetric pattern: mothers reported increased daily enjoyment when fathers assumed a moderate share of childcare, while fathers experienced higher levels of enjoyment only when their involvement was more substantial. Both maternal and paternal SWB were also influenced by the total amount of time parents dedicated to childcare. These results underscore the importance of both the distribution and intensity of childcare in shaping parental well-being.

Keywords: Childcare, division of labour, well-being, parents, enjoyment, time use survey

Introduction

In recent years, policymakers have renewed their focus on how caregiving responsibilities are divided between men and women in heterosexual families, and how these roles might be rebalanced. One of the key motivations for this interest is the impact these divisions have on the perpetuation of the gender wage gap, which is underpinned by the unequal distribution of unpaid labour (Government Equalities Office 2019). The enduring notion of the “second shift”—where women continue to shoulder most domestic and caregiving responsibilities—limits their ability to compete on equal terms with men in the labour market (Hochschild 1989). Motherhood and the adjustment women make to their employment patterns – namely dropping out of the labour market or moving into part-time jobs/reducing working hours – is considered one of the biggest contributors of the gender pay gap in the UK [CostaDias2018].

While fathers are spending more time with their children than in previous generations (Craig, Powell, and Smyth 2014; Gracia and Esping-Andersen 2015) - a trend amplified by the COVID-19 pandemic — substantial gender disparities in childcare persist, particularly during a child’s early years (Fatherhood Institute 2016; ONS 2020). At the same time, an increasing number of fathers, especially the younger generations, express a desire to be more actively involved in caregiving, even at the expense of their earnings or career opportunities (Working Families 2017; Burgess and Goldman 2021).

Nonetheless, entrenched gender norms continue to cast men as breadwinners and women as primary caregivers (Scott 2013; Taylor 2018). Many fathers remain hesitant to increase their caregiving role due to concerns about potential negative impacts on their careers (Kelland, Lewis, and Fisher 2022; Rudman and Mescher 2013). Similarly, women’s engagement in paid work is still shaped by traditional beliefs, such as the perception that maternal employment negatively affects young children and family life (Scott 2013; J. Allen and Stevenson 2023). To investigate such deeply held norms, it is essential to scrutinise the perceptions of paternal involvement in childcare and its consequences.

A growing body of research has begun to do so by examining the benefits of father involvement in childcare for both children’s outcomes and the parental relationship (Allen and Daly 2007; Chung 2021; Huerta et al. 2014; Kroll et al. 2016; K. Nomaguchi, Brown, and Leyman 2017; Norman, Elliot, and Fagan 2018; Norman and Davies 2023; Schober 2012, 2013; Twamley et al. 2013). However, many of these studies focus narrowly on specific caregiving tasks, often linked to the child’s age. Such approaches not only overlook the actual amount of time fathers spend on childcare, but also how it is shared between parents.

Even among studies that use more fine-grained time-use data (e.g. Baxter and Smart 2011; Vagni 2022), few distinguish between categories of care such as routine vs. non-routine, or solo vs. joint caregiving. Yet these distinctions are crucial for identifying the conditions under which fathers take on a primary caregiving role - an essential contribution for supporting maternal labour market participation.

Furthermore, while some studies have explored the implications of childcare arrangements for parental well-being, most rely on cognitive measures such as life satisfaction (e.g. Glass, Andersson, and Simon 2016; Schober and Stahl 2016) , which may not capture short- or medium-term fluctuations due to day-to-day variation in caregiving demands. Much of this research also focuses on negative outcomes - relationship strain, stress, or poor well-being (e.g. Frisco and Williams 2003; Milkie et al. 2002; K. Nomaguchi, Brown, and Leyman 2017; Norman, Elliot, and Fagan 2018; Ruppanner, Brandén, and Turunen 2018; Schober 2012, 2013) - rather than investigating the broader, mutual effects of caregiving on parents’ subjective well-being.

Only recently has there been some efforts made to measure more momentary positive affects, such as meaning, happiness, enjoyment explored on a day-to-day or moment-to-moment basis (e.g. McDonnell, Luke, and Short 2019; Meier et al. 2018; Roeters and Gracia 2016) largely using American Time Use Survey data. These studies have shown how time spent with children results in varying levels of momentary positive affects, depending on your gender (mothers vs fathers), the activities taken place (routine vs interactive/play/educational), and the length of the time spent (shorter vs longer time periods). However, they also do not consider how the division of care shapes these outcomes.

This study addresses these gaps by examining how the time fathers spend on childcare, and the division of care among heterosexual couples, are associated with short-term fluctuations in parental well-being. We do so by using daily enjoyment measures from time-diaries collected in the 2015 UK Time Use Survey. Our analysis focuses on how different types of childcare are distributed and experienced. Based on previous studies (e.g. McDonnell, Luke, and Short 2019; Meier et al. 2018; Roeters and Gracia 2016; Vagni 2022) we distinguish between routine vs. enrichment activities as well as whether the care is carried out by oneself (solo) or jointly, which has been shown to impact parental affect when providing care.

We do so using an innovative measure - daily enjoyment—from time-diaries collected in the 2015 UK Time Use Survey. Background

Types of Care

Craig and Mullan (2011) have argued that measuring total caregiving time alone is insufficient to understand the gendered dynamics of childcare, making the case that it is essential to distinguish between types of care and caregiving contexts. Additionally, childcare can occur as a primary or secondary activity, such as while performing housework or paid work, and both forms must be considered (Craig and Powell 2011).

Research consistently shows that mothers perform more childcare overall and are more likely to be responsible for routine care, whereas fathers tend to engage more in non-routine or enrichment activities (Craig 2006; Craig and Powell 2011; Craig and Mullan 2011). Routine care includes physical and scheduled activities such as feeding, bathing, bedtime routines, and school drop-offs, which often conflict with rigid work hours. Non-routine care involves play, reading, and educational activities that tend to fit more flexibly into daily schedules. This division has gendered implications, as it is only when fathers take on a significant share of routine care that mothers can be relieved of the time pressures that constrain their participation in paid employment [Chung2018].

In this study, we focus on childcare carried out by a parent alone. Joint caregiving does not free the other parent to engage in alternative activities, and research suggests that in such settings, mothers often retain primary responsibility (Craig 2006). As such, the mental and emotional burden—or “mental load” - continues to fall disproportionately on mothers, even in the context of shared tasks (Dean, Churchill, and Ruppanner 2022; Walzer 1996).

Care giving and subjective well-being

In this paper, we distinguish between evaluative or cognitive subjective well-being (SWB) – i.e. life satisfaction, happiness, satisfaction of work-life balance etc., of parents from the momentary or affective measures of subjective well-being – i.e., how much enjoyment, happiness, stress the individual feels whilst doing that specific activity. This is because whereas parenting may contribute to meaning and enjoyment of one’s day to day life, it may also potentially increase the long-term stress or decrease one’s life satisfaction. It is important to distinguish between these two types not only because they are measuring different dimensions of SWB, distinguishing between the longer term versus shorter term elements of SWB, but also because the evidence behind parenting and SWB outcomes are mixed depending on what is being measured.

Much of the early literature on parenthood and well-being has relied on cognitive measures of SWB in both positive - namely happiness, life satisfaction, and negative - stress levels, time pressure. Many of these earlier studies link parenthood with negative SWB outcomes or null effects (Hansen 2012; Glass, Andersson, and Simon 2016; Myrskylä and Margolis 2014). Some studies have also shown that there is in fact a positive impact of children on parental well-being especially in a longer-term perspective, but only when factors such as financial and time costs are taken into account (Pollmann-Schult 2014). Mothers in particular report significant declines in life satisfaction or increased levels of stress following childbirth. This is often linked to the gendered nature of caregiving especially in the early stages of a child’s life (Hochschild 1989; Walzer 1996), where mothers are the ones who are largely seen responsible to meet the demands of increased caregiving, which leads to career sacrifices increased work-family conflict levels (Chandola et al. 2019; K. M. Nomaguchi and Milkie 2003; Ruppanner, Brandén, and Turunen 2018; Yucel and Chung 2023). This unequal division of domestic labour not only decreases the mother’s subjective and objective well-being levels directly, but also may increase feelings of unfairness when division of labour is unequal(Petts, Kaufman, and Mize 2023; Schieman, Ruppanne, and Milkie 2018), which can further lead to negative outcomes for SWB. In contrast, fathers tend to report more stable or even positive levels of life satisfaction (Hansen 2012; Kohler, Behrman, and Skytthe 2005). Studies found that parenthood and caregiving, especially for fathers, can foster a sense of purpose and recognition (K. M. Nomaguchi and Milkie 2003) and that involved fathers also report higher job satisfaction, better work-family balance, and lower work-family conflict (Ladge et al. 2015).

Despite the large number of papers linking parenthood with potential decline in SWB compared to non-parents, an increasing number of studies have also linked caregiving as an act with positive momentary affects such as meaning, enjoyment, and happiness (e.g., McDonnell, Luke, and Short 2019; Meier et al. 2018; Roeters and Gracia 2016). Research using American Time Use Survey data has shown that parents experience greater happiness during time spent in the presence of children, compared to non-parents during similar activities indicating that childcare may enhance affective well-being of parents (Negraia and Augustine 2020). This result is mirrored in the study by Vagni (2022) who found that joint time with parent and child also yields high enjoyment levels. Other studies, also using the American Time Use Survey, show how parents experience high levels of happiness and meaning whilst providing childcare (McDonnell, Luke, and Short 2019; Meier et al. 2018; Roeters and Gracia 2016). Studies show how this may be especially true when parents are providing enrichment care – namely interactive, recreational, play centered activities. Roeters and Gracia (2016) further argue that it is because interactive care is perceived as more meaningful and less stressful than routine care.

The positive affective outcomes of caregiving, however, differ significantly between mothers and fathers. Several studies (McDonnell, Luke, and Short 2019; Meier et al. 2018; Roeters and Gracia 2016; Walzer 1996) found that the emotional benefits of caregiving—such as increased happiness and meaning—are typically stronger for fathers than for mothers. This gender gap is often attributed to the division of childcare tasks, where fathers are more likely to engage in recreational or interactive care, such as playing or reading, while mothers tend to perform more routine and time-sensitive tasks, like feeding, supervising, or organizing [McDonnell, Luke, and Short (2019); Meier et al. (2018). The amount of time parents spend in childcare also shapes their affective experiences: since mothers generally spend more time in caregiving overall, including multitasking and solo care (Vagni 2022), they are more exposed to stress and fatigue, which may reduce their experience of positive affect whilst caregiving. In contrast, fathers’ parenting time is often more limited and concentrated in enjoyable activities, contributing to more consistently positive affective experiences and thus explaining the persistent gender differences in how childcare relates to subjective well-being. This leads us to believe that the care giving context is important when considering its impact on SWB outcomes for parents. The context we focus on is the division of care between parents, a relatively neglected area of study.

Sharing childcare and parental well-being

There are a number of ways in which share of care may help support parent’s well-being outcomes. Previous studies have shown how the reduction of SWB for parents is largely due to the pressures of having to meet both parenting and work demands. It is especially in countries where there is lack of support for parents where we see a greater association between parenthood and decline in SWB outcomes (Glass, Andersson, and Simon 2016; Myrskylä and Margolis 2014). This is especially true for mothers who generally carry out a larger share of the childcare and are generally considered to be more responsible for childcare and housework. We can therefore expect a more equitable sharing of childcare responsibilities can reduce the time pressure and stress mothers experience, contributing to improved well-being. Given what we know about the association between different childcare activities and parental SWB explored in the previous section, we expect this to be especially true for routine care. As interactive, enrichment care may also provide positive affect (Roeters and Gracia 2016; Meier et al. 2018), and because they already take a relatively larger part in these activities compared to routine care, fathers taking on a larger share of enrichment non-routine activities may not be as impactful. Sharing care may also influence parental well-being by shaping parental relationship satisfaction (Petts, Kaufman, and Mize 2023). Existing research links unequal divisions of childcare to lower relationship satisfaction and higher rates of marital dissolution (Carlson, Hanson, and Fitzroy 2016; Kalmijn 1999; Norman, Elliot, and Fagan 2018; Schober 2012), which has a significant influence in well-being outcomes for both partners. The link between shared care and parental well-being via relationship satisfaction is likely to influence both fathers and mothers’ SWB outcomes.

An equal sharing of childcare may also enhance father’s well-being outcomes as well. As previously mentioned, studies (Meier et al. 2018; Roeters and Gracia 2016) have shown greater involvement in childcare enhances fathers’ feelings of purpose and happiness, while fathers’ well-being can decline when they perceive a lack of autonomy, feel excluded from caregiving decisions (Ruppanner, Brandén, and Turunen 2018). What is more, as father’s involvement levels are generally lower, their involvement is less likely to impact their careers or clash with their work responsibilities (Kaufman and Uhlenberg 2000). However, similar to mothers, this effect may wear off as fathers take part in a larger share of childcare where we may encounter a more negative impact on father’s well-being outcomes. Similar to mothers, we expect enrichment activities, typically play-based and enjoyable, to be more positively associated with father’s SWB. This may be especially true as these activities align more closely with emerging representations of nurturing masculinity (Kvande and Brandth 2017). In contrast, fathers’ engagement in routine caregiving may not yield the same emotional benefits and may still be perceived as a “feminine” responsibility.

Summary And Hypotheses

Our overall hypothesis is that a greater the share of childcare performed by fathers, is associated with higher well-being for both mothers and fathers, particularly in terms of their daily enjoyment levels. We expect when childcare, especially routine care, is more equally shared, mothers’ well-being improves, as it reduces their time pressure and stress. Given that enrichment care is enjoyable also for the mothers and given that it is one area that is already relatively shared, fathers increased share in this childcare may not make as much of a difference. . For fathers, we anticipate that increased involvement in childcare, particularly in enrichment or non-routine activities, will be associated with greater enjoyment and subjective well-being. However, drawing on prior research suggesting that fathers’ positive affect may only increase up to a certain threshold, beyond which childcare begins to conflict with work or other responsibilities, we hypothesize a non-linear, U-shaped association between fathers’ caregiving share and their well-being.

H1. The greater the share of childcare taken on by fathers, the higher the mothers’ subjective well-being.

H1a. This effect is stronger when fathers take on a larger share of routine childcare.

H2. The greater the share of childcare taken on by fathers, the higher the fathers’ subjective well-being.

H2a. This effect is stronger when fathers take on a larger share of enrichment (non-routine) childcare.

H2b. This effect is limited to moderate levels of involvement and diminishes or reverses when fathers take on equal or more of total childcare responsibilities.

Data And Methods

Data and sample

We draws on the 2015 UK Time Use Survey (TUS), a two-stage stratified random sample survey representative of non-institutional households across the UK. Data were collected through self-completed time diaries—coded using the HETUS classification—and face-to-face individual questionnaires. Diaries recorded primary and secondary activities in 10-minute intervals over two 24-hour periods (one weekday and one weekend day). Respondents also documented the location of activities, co-presence with others, and their level of enjoyment. Diary days were randomly assigned throughout the year and were the same for all members of the same household.

The overall dataset includes 11,421 individuals from 4,733 households, of which 8,210 individuals across 3,606 households completed both time diaries. The analytical sample focuses on diary days in 2014–2015 from UK households comprising two cohabiting heterosexual adults, both in paid employment, with at least one child aged 0–14. At least one parent must have reported childcare activities in their diary. The analysis is conducted at the household level using matched data for mothers and fathers. Single-parent households and multi-family or multigenerational households were excluded to minimise confounding by non-parental caregiving and as the focus of our analysis lies in the division of care shared by parents.

The resulting analytic sample includes 852 valid diary days from 428 households with complete information on childcare and well-being. While the relatively small sample size limits statistical power for multivariate analysis, it remains a valid representative sample from which inferences to the days of UK households can be made, albeit with caution.

Dependent Variables

  • Overall daily enjoyment This measure of experienced well-being was recorded in the time-diary using a 7-point scale. We operationalised it as the average enjoyment score across all non-resting activities for a full day. We chose to focus on overall daily enjoyment rather than enjoyment disaggregated by care type due to sample size constraints. As this measure was only introduced in October 2014 after fieldwork had already started, approximately 25% of cases lack enjoyment data.

  • Evaluative well-being and life satisfaction In order for our results to remain comparable with other studies, we also used more traditional measures of cognitive well-being:

    • Life Satisfaction: “Overall, how satisfied are you with your life nowadays?” (0 = Not at all, 10 = Completely). Due to a mid-fieldwork change in the scale (from 7 to 10 points), we standardised and merged both versions to maximise sample size;
    • Work-Life Balance Satisfaction: “How dissatisfied or satisfied are you with your work-life/work-family balance?” (1–7 scale);
    • Social Life Satisfaction: “How dissatisfied or satisfied are you with your social life?” (1–7 scale).

These facets are particularly relevant to parenting and caregiving contexts.

Independent variables

Fathers’ share of childcare The main explanatory variable captures the percentage of total childcare time (across both parents) provided by fathers on a given day. We distinguish between:

  • Routine Care: Feeding, bathing, dressing, soothing, transport to/from school.
  • Enrichment/Non-Routine Care: Talking, reading, playing, storytelling, educational engagement as proposed by Craig and Powell (2011)
  • Solo Care: Provided when the other parent is not present.
  • Primary Care: Recorded as the main activity, regardless of secondary activities.

For each type, we calculated the father’s minutes spent on caregiving as a share of the total caregiving minutes within the household. To capture potential non-linear effects, we created an ordinal variable with three categories:

  1. Fathers performed no childcare (reference group)
  2. Fathers performed <40% of total care
  3. Fathers performed ≥40% of total care

We chose a 40% threshold instead of 50% to increase the number of observations in the upper category, given the low prevalence of fathers taking on the majority of care. Table 1 provides a description of these different types in our data. Solo care was defined as care provided the parent when the other parent was not present at the same time.

For each one of these types, the number of minutes fathers spent caring was divided by the total caring time spent by mothers and fathers within each household then multiplied by 100. In order to account for the likely nonlinear effect of this variable on well-being, we derived a three-category ordinal version consisting of: fathers not doing any childcare on diary day (reference category); fathers taking on less than 40% of the childcare; fathers taking on 40% or more of the childcare. The cutting point was set at 40 % rather than 50% in order to alleviate the small number of fathers caring more than 50% of the time.

Modelling Strategy

We examined both bivariate and multivariate associations between paternal share of childcare and parental well-being outcomes, broken down by type of care. As each respondent contributed two diary days, we estimated OLS regressions with robust standard errors using the estimatr package in R (Blair et al. (2024)).

We controlled for the socio-demographic characteristics of respondents, as they are known to influence the time spent on childcare (Craig and Powell (2011)) and well-being (Kroll et al. (2016); Milkie et al. (2002); Schober (2012)). Control variables included:

  • Parental education
  • Household employment configuration (dual full-time, one full-/one part-time)
  • Age of each parent
  • Day type (weekday vs weekend)
  • Fathers’ absolute time spent on childcare (to separate the effect of sharing from quantity of care)

We included this last control to account for the possibility that the effect of care share may differ depending on whether a day involved high or low caregiving demands for the mother.

Results

On average, the respondents in our sample tend to be more educated than the general population, with mothers more highly educated than fathers. This discrepancy is likely explained by the higher educational attainment among employed mothers compared to those who are economically inactive. Notably, and perhaps unsurprisingly, there is a gendered divide in access to flexible working arrangements: 31.1% of households have mothers working under some form of flexible hours (18.1% full-time and 13% part-time), compared to only 12.7% of fathers (7.6% full-time and 5.1% part-time). In 35.2% of households, mothers served as secondary earners while fathers worked full-time, compared to 46.2% of households where both parents were in full-time employment. Finally, 81% of households had at least one child below primary school age—unsurprising given the study’s design, which required at least one episode of childcare to be recorded in the diary. Consequently, households with children over the age of 10 were rare.

Table 3 provides an initial overview of how childcare responsibilities were distributed between mothers and fathers. In 29.7% of cases, fathers did not report participating in any form of childcare on the diary day. However, 70.4% reported engaging in primary childcare, with roughly one-third taking on 40% or more of the total care. Interestingly, 62% of fathers participated in both routine and enriched care activities - a pattern likely influenced by the dual-earner composition of the sample, characterised by higher levels of qualification than the general population, which tends to correlate with higher levels of paternal involvement in childcare overall (see also Dotti Sani and Treas (2016)).

As expected, no category of childcare showed a majority of fathers taking on more time than mothers. The proportion of fathers reporting at least 40% of total childcare time ranged from 20.3% for solo care to 36% for joint care. Fathers were slightly more likely to provide 40% or more of enriched and solo care (36.8% and 26.9%, respectively) compared to providing less than 40% (26.5% and 20.3%, respectively). These patterns varied depending on whether the diary was recorded on a weekday or weekend. For instance, 25.4% of fathers reported no childcare on weekdays, compared to 18.6% on weekends (data not shown).

Multivariate Analysis

Overview

Initial analyses revealed no significant positive association between either mothers’ or fathers’ well-being and the proportion of childcare undertaken by fathers. In contrast, several negative associations were identified on days when fathers accounted for more than 40% of the total childcare time. Overall, models performed better in predicting daily enjoyment and life satisfaction than social life or work-life balance satisfaction.

The absolute time fathers spent on childcare, rather than their share relative to mothers, was weakly positively associated with their well-being. When interaction terms were introduced - examining how fathers’ share of care intersected with each parent’s total care time - a more nuanced picture emerged, particularly for fathers.

Mothers

Table 5 indicates no clear evidence that an increased paternal share of childcare correlates positively with mothers’ well-being, after adjusting for socio-demographic factors and total childcare time. Unexpectedly, maternal daily enjoyment was lower in households where fathers undertook more than 40% of solo care (-.25, \(p<.05\)). A similar pattern was observed for life satisfaction when fathers provided 40% or more of primary care (-.40, \(p<.05\)). A larger share of solo care by fathers’ also negatively affected mothers’ life satisfaction, although this finding was only significant at the p<.1 level.

Some weakly significant positive associations were identified: for example, fathers’ time spent on primary and routine childcare was positively related to mothers’ life satisfaction (.16 and .12 \(p<.1\), respectively). Prior to controlling for covariates, mothers reported higher daily enjoyment when fathers provided 1–40% of primary childcare (results available upon request), suggesting that moderate paternal involvement may be more beneficial than extensive involvement.

Role of control variables

Comparing models with and without control variables highlighted several patterns. Controlling for whether caring occurred during a weekday or at the weekend reduces the coefficient and significance of enrichment care on well-being: in other words, the positive impact of fathers spending time on enriched care might in fact be partly due to the fact that it predominantly occurs at the weekend, a time during which daily enjoyment tends to be higher for all.

These findings suggest that greater paternal involvement can enhance maternal enjoyment, but only when mothers themselves are also highly involved in childcare. Conversely, fathers report higher enjoyment when their involvement is more modest relative to their partner.

Discussion And Conclusion

This paper has examined the relationship between the division of childcare and parental well-being among dual-earner couples with children, drawing on detailed time-use diary data. Unlike previous studies that rely on perceptions or self-reported frequencies, this approach allowed us to investigate both the actual amount and relative share of childcare time undertaken by fathers. We also explored different types of care (routine, enrichment, primary, solo) and employed an innovative measure of immediate well-being: daily enjoyment, alongside conventional metrics such as life satisfaction and work-life balance.

An important result is that despite increasing societal expectations around father involvement, nearly one-third of fathers in our sample did not report any childcare on the diary day. Moreover, close to half had no solo care responsibilities. Only a minority of fathers undertook an equal or greater share of childcare.

Contrary to some expectations, we did not find consistent evidence that increased paternal involvement improved maternal well-being. In some cases, mothers reported lower enjoyment and life satisfaction when fathers took on a larger share of care. For fathers themselves, the relationship between childcare and well-being was more complex: while high shares of routine or solo care were linked to lower life satisfaction, greater involvement in enrichment and primary care was associated with higher daily enjoyment.

Unpacking these associations by interacting absolute and relative involvement in childcare clarified that a sustained involvement in primary childcare by fathers was associated with higher daily enjoyment among them - even if that may not be the case for specific, less gratifying kinds of care, such as routine childcare. Indeed, similarly to previous studies, we found that fathers enjoyed their day more as they were more intensively involved in enrichment childcare. It may also be that that the negative association is the result of a reverse relationship: some families in which fathers experienced lower life satisfaction, were those where they were taking a larger share of childcare.

These findings suggest that moderate, meaningful involvement - particularly in enrichment childcare - may be most beneficial for fathers. Conversely, a large share of less enjoyable caregiving tasks may negatively affect well-being.

This leads us to an interesting conclusion. Much of the discussion around the sharing of childcare so far has been driven by its impact on gender equality, namely how a more equal distribution of caring roles can help women fully compete with men in the commodified labour market. However, as this study and some previous studies have shown (Ladge et al. (2015); Negraia and Augustine (2020)), getting fathers more involved in childcare may also improve men’s (mental) health. Masculinities and men’s identities are shifting as society’s ideas of gender roles change and demands of men and fathers change (Brandth and Kvande (1998); Lyonette and Crompton (2015); Elliott (2016)). What our study has shown is that we need to think about men’s involvement in childcare as being instrumental to improve their well-being. Men should be allowed to expand their roles as carers not only to ensure that they are able to meet their duties as involved fathers, and equal partners in parenting, but also because it benefits their mental health, and as a result, possibly their overall health and well-being in the long term as well.

These findings also contribute to the literature on maternal well-being. While past research has largely focused on cognitive measures, our study adds emotional and experiential insights. Daily enjoyment, in particular, appears to be more sensitive to changes in childcare division than broader life satisfaction measures. Our study shows that father’s involvement in childcare has a positive impact on the immediate well-being - daily enjoyment - of both parents (see also Negraia and Augustine (2020)).

The paper is not without its limitations: the relatively small sample size, which constrains statistical power, and the simplicity of the daily enjoyment measure, which does not capture negative affects or arousal (see Warr (2007)). Future research should replicate these findings with larger samples and improved instruments. Policy implications are substantial. As governments and organizations promote paternity leave and flexible work, our study suggests these initiatives should be framed not only around economic productivity or gender equity, but also around enhancing parental well-being. Fathers who are more involved in childcare may become happier, more balanced, and ultimately more productive workers.

These findings have significant implications for government and corporate policies.

Some governments have been pushing for greater involvement by fathers in the care of their children, for instance, by increasing the size of the fathers-only portion of parental leave (see also the European Commission’s Work-life balance directive). Much of these tend to aim to increase the size of the female workforce and fully exploit the female human capital potential. This paper shows that the conversation also needs to focus on enhancing parental well-being of fathers and men’s in addition to that of mothers. This can reinforce the case for implementing such policies: which are not only necessary for sustaining economic growth or for women’s rights per se, but also to enhance the well-being of both men and women.

Our results also have implications for companies and managers. We know from the organizational studies literature that workers who are happy and have better work-life balance will be more productive in companies (Leslie et al. (2012)). Whilst retaining female workers is considered to be the main raison d’être of fathers’ involvement in childcare, this may not provide sufficient incentives for employers of fathers. Increased maternal labour force participation may benefit other companies. However, as we can see from the results of our study, supporting fathers to play a larger role in the care of children can improve their well-being which can directly relate to worker’s productivity.

Finally, our study carries an important message to fathers and mothers. Fathers may be reluctant to use leaves and flexible working arrangements to be more involved in the care of their children due to the stigma it carries (Rudman and Mescher (2013)), and for fear of experiencing disadvantage in their career. Lastly, for families, our study offers an encouraging message: sharing caregiving more equally can lead to more satisfying, emotionally rich days for both mothers and fathers. Shifting toward greater co-parenting is not just good for gender equality—it may also be good for everyone’s well-being.
